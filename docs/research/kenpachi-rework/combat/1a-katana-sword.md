# 1a. How the katana and the sword move: real martial arts and the screen (for Kenpachi's move modules)

Scope: one-handed sword use (iai one-hand cuts, the kodachi, the messer, the sabre, Musashi's one-hand doctrine), two-handed
katana (kendo, kamae, koryū such as Jigen-ryū, the Liechtenauer longsword), the phases and tells of a cut, measured timings,

## (a) Narrative by sub-topic

### 1. One-handed sword and katana

- **Musashi argues for one hand, and says when to switch to two.** His Ground Book: with both hands "it is difficult to wield it
  freely to left and right", so he carries the sword in one hand; when one hand cannot cut the enemy down, use both. This
  maps directly onto base/KATATE (one hand, wide lateral freedom) against RYOTE (both hands, the cut one hand can't finish).
  He also warns against one-handed "short sword chopping" with a long blade: swing it broadly and calmly, "always stretching the
  elbows broadly". Read the other way round, that error is a ready-made signature for a schoolless brawler.
- **The messer (Lecküchner) keeps the free hand in play.** The text opens by telling you to fence one-handed with the
  other hand "on the back", turning it to the chest to grab, take the blade or clench the arms. Its first cut, the Zornhau, is
  "nothing else but a simple peasant stroke". This gives KATATE its punching / grabbing free hand, and gives the base form a
  canonical "untrained" cut.
- **Iai: the draw-cut opens, single-hand cuts are light, kirioroshi finishes** ("the final positive act of cutting"). The Seitei *Mae* kata draws into a
  horizontal cut to the head, then a straight cut down (kirioroshi). An iai teacher's notes say single-handed kirioroshi suits a small, shallow target,
  or a cut made on the way to the main target. Kamimoto sensei separates *nukitsuke* (a draw that forestalls the attack) from
  *nukiuchi* (a draw that cuts the opponent down). The kodachi (Niten Ichi-ryū's short-sword set) works with the free hand: the
  left hand rests on the hip, then grabs the opponent's arm or elbow before the stab.
- **The one-handed sabre:** a small kinematic study (n = 6) found blade-tip speed tracks the elbow's angle
  change (r = −0.83), not the wrist's. An old-Polish-sabre study calls wrist work essential for control of the opponent's
  blade. So one-handed cutting splits into elbow-driven power cuts and wrist-driven control cuts.
- **The wild "no school" swordsman on screen:** Kurosawa's Kikuchiyo (Seven Samurai) is read as a kabuki *aragoto* figure with
  an ōdachi, striking *mie* poses. Zatoichi (Kitano 2003) shows the opposite pole: economy, a reverse draw for close quarters,
  and no wire-work acrobatics.

### 2. Two-handed katana: kendo, kamae, koryū, and the longsword for comparison

- **Kendo's strikes are measured.** An LED-cued men strike from 2.30 m (about the match maai) took 1.031 ± 0.105 s from cue to hit
  in 20 university players. Ranking correlated with the **upswing** phase time but **not** the downswing (r = −0.154, n.s.): skill
  shows in how fast the windup and the entry happen, not in the drop. Elite match footage puts the most successful attacks at
  0.09–0.15 s from the start of the forward / downward shinai motion to impact. [60 Hz: about 5–9 f for that release.]
- **Kendo strikes stop on the target; iai cuts go through.** "In Kendo the Shinai stops as it hits the target", while in iai the
  cut carries to the finishing position. A cut stopped at the target reaches impact about 25–35 ms later than a through-cut.
  This gives two hitstop / follow-through profiles: RYOTE taps and holds (zanshin), the wild forms cut through and drag.
- **Seme → tame → one motion.** Seme is the unrelenting pressure; tame is "active waiting", with the exhale suspended (not
  halted), then the move in to strike "in one single motion". Tanaka sensei: advanced players take longer over five men strikes
  than beginners. FIK rules require a valid strike to be "followed by zanshin", and void it if the striker shows "excessive
  excitement". Kendo's own grammar is therefore stillness, a burst, then composure, the inverse of a berserker.
- **The kamae:** jōdan (sword overhead, the "most aggressive" stance, weak defence, maai hard to judge, rarely one-handed),
  hassō (hilt by the right shoulder, raised to jōdan to cut), waki (blade hidden behind the body, "no hint" of the next attack).
  Katsugi-waza deliberately makes a big shoulder lift to startle the opponent into an opening. Renzoku-waza chains kote-men or
  kote-men-dō, each strike fully executed.
- **Koryū (Jigen-ryū):** the tonbo stance (right hand high beside the head). The attack runs in and cuts diagonally down at the
  neck; "a second strike is not even to be considered"; the kiai is "Ei!". Its tategi-uchi drill approaches a post from about 9 m
  and strikes left and right over and over. This is a two-handed style that reads as a charge, not as kendo. It suits NOMIHOSE
  and the Bankai more than RYOTE.
- **The German longsword:** four guards only (Ochs, Pflug, Vom Tag, Alber), with the gloss "He who lies still, is dead".
  Meyer's Zornhau starts high in the Wrathful Guard, passes Long Point midway and ends in the Changer, "strongest beyond all
  others". Hassō equals right Vom Tag.

### 3. The phases of a cut, weight, feet, distance, and the tell

- **Phases (a measured longsword cut, 100 fps):** an initial arc where "mainly the body starts to move and the blade moves
  little" (**this is the tell**), an acceleration arc of 30–40°, a constant-speed arc under 50 ms, then a deceleration arc.
- **Explosive vs standard cuts:** explosive arm-thrown cuts last 250–350 ms, are not in sync with a step and lose reach. Standard
  cuts are synced to a step (Meyer: "Every cut needs to be done with a matching step"), last 600 ms or more, gain 10–20 cm
  of reach, and give the opponent "ample warning". For a high-energy cut, "if such a cut misses, it takes long to recover". [60 Hz: 15–21 f against
  36+ f.]
- **The feet:** in kendo the strike is driven by lifting the right foot quickly and pushing off hard with the left. Slow strikers
  "shifted their weight from the left foot to the right foot and back to the left foot" before lifting the right foot, a
  visible shuffle. In basic strikes the kensaki moves first; in battle strikes "the right foot moves before the kensaki". Silver
  (1599) calls hand-led times true and foot-led times false (the foot alone is too slow to answer the hand).
- **Where the tell shows:** Sakudo sensei tells you to watch where intent appears: "their shinai top? their right hand? their
  eyes?". The iai draw teaches the opposite skill, hiding it: the hand moving to the tsuka "must not look big".
- **Reaction floor:** Sucker Punch (Zimmerman) states about 0.3 s to respond to a visual stimulus. The longsword measurements put human
  reaction at about 250 ms. [60 Hz: 15–18 f. The module rule "tell ≥ 14 f" sits just under this floor, so a 14 f tell can
  only be reacted to if the player is already watching for it.]

### 5. Screen and game direction

- **Rurouni Kenshin (Tanigaki Kenji, action director):** the reverse-blade sakabatō lets the hero hit as often as he likes,
  because "the opponent does not die with one shot". A real blade forced him to think about "how to cut": a shallow slice, or
  a blade "caught in the meat once" and then forced through. "As the number of steps increases, it becomes less real"
  Director Ohtomo: drama : action is usually 7 : 3 in his scripts; for this film he reversed it (action 7, the rest 3).
- **Kurosawa's rhythm (Bordwell via UW Cinematheque):** actors stand still "for long periods of time, sword raised, before a blow
  is struck"; "Rapid pacing requires the foil of extreme immobility" The Yoshio Sugino-choreographed Seven Samurai is credited
  with bringing "dynamism and reality" to staid sword fights.
- **Ghost of Tsushima (Sucker Punch, Chris Zimmerman):** "a handful of cuts is enough to fell the toughest enemy". Realistic
  mocap speeds were too fast to react to, so the first attack of a string is reactable and the rest "happen fast". Attacks
  cancel at any point. Stances each answer a class of enemy.
- **Sekiro (Miyazaki):** "intensity" comes from the clash of steel, "clang, clang, clang".
- **Onimusha: Way of the Sword (Capcom, animation director Hiroshi Yugen):** a "very realistic, grounded sword action" goal,
  built with sword choreographers from Katsugekiza Motion Capture Studio. This is process only, with no timing data.
- **A synthesis for "no school"** (not a single source): Musashi's "short sword chopping", Silver's foot-led false times,
  Murase's weight shuffle and Lecküchner's "peasant stroke" together describe the untrained swordsman. He windmills a long blade
  with short arcs, leads with the feet and body (readable early), shifts his weight before he goes, and relies on one crude
  strong cut from above.

## Inferences for the move modules (mine, not sources')

- **Separation axis 1, the grip:** one hand = lateral, wide, choppy, body-first, cut-through (rows 1, 2, 5, 17, 18). Two hands =
  vertical, compact, hand-first, stopped with zanshin (rows 3, 18, 22, 25).
- **Separation axis 2, the tempo shape:** RYOTE = stillness (tame), one fast release, a held finish (rows 9, 10, 22, 25, 31).
  NOMIHOSE = an unbroken stream of alternating left / right hammer cuts with no guard pose (rows 14, 31, 33). Bankai = a run-in,
  one huge cut that catches and drags, a mie finish (rows 11, 13, 21, 47).
- **The slow-to-fast rule:** only the first segment of a string needs the full reactable tell; later segments may be faster
  (row 7). A uniform-length string reads as "off" (row 12).
- **Tell placement:** use the body-first initial arc, the weight shuffle, the shoulder lift (rows 4, 20, 28), and the three
  channels of blade tip, right hand and eyes (row 30). In waki or hidden-draw stances the blade can't carry the tell (rows 36, 38).
