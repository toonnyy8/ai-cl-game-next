# 1b. How heavy blades and one-armed fighting move: references for Kenpachi's forms

> Verified 2026-11-02 (`verify.md`: KEEP 208, FIX 7, DROP 1; the rows it did not reach are listed there). The FIX / DROP items are applied in this copy.

Scope: greatsword / montante / ōdachi, the one-hand cleaver or axe with a working free hand, fighting with one arm, how
animators and game designers sell weight, and swing biomechanics. Bleach canon, Bleach games and per-move frame tables
are left to the other researchers. Every claim cites the URL it was read on. Quotes were checked against the raw page
text unless the row says they are paraphrased. Grades: A = primary (period manual, measured study, creator), B =
published or official secondary, C = wiki or fan.

Forms: base (notched katana, one hand, no school), KATATE (huge cleaver in one hand, the free hand punches), RYOTE
(two-handed kendo), NOMIHOSE (berserk, fast and heavy), Bankai (broken huge blade used like a crude axe or club),
KATAUDE (one arm only).

## (a) Narrative by sub-topic

### 1. Greatsword, montante and ōdachi in real use

The best primary source on a heavy two-hander that keeps moving is Diogo Gomes de Figueyredo's *Memorial da Prattica do
Montante* (1651), in the Myers/Hick translation on Wiktenauer. Four of its rules matter for move design:

- **The body turns with every cut.** "the body must always turn toward where the montante cuts". All montante actions
  "must be helped by the movements of the body".
- **Cuts alternate and circle on a pivot foot.** Drills alternate talho and revez in each step "until the people stop".
  "Circling" cuts pass the blade over the head and behind the shoulders. One foot stays planted and "serves as an axis
  for the body".
- **Where one cut ends decides the next.** "all the thrusts which are readied over the right arm originate from the end
  of talhos". Revezes lead into the left-arm and pommel thrusts.
- **The end is planted, and a miss is dangerous.** Each blow ends with the body steady. If not, you "could dangerously
  fall, either for not being well and firmly planted, or by missing".

The Fly-Swatter is the one standing flurry: two talhos and two revezes "while standing still and with the left arm held
firmly against the body". The rules are written for crowds ("a wide road with people in front and behind").

Roger Norling (HROARR) links the montante to Meyer's halberd spin. The fighter turns fully around and strikes "with one
hand, going around". He calls it "a very powerful one-hand strike" that "momentarily exposes the one who performs it".
Taken together, the montante looks like the clearest model for NOMIHOSE: one continuous wheel, each cut setting up the
next, a planted pivot, and punishment on a whiff.

Real greatswords were not as heavy as fiction makes them. Wikipedia gives the zweihänder 2–4 kg, typically about 2 kg at
1.4 m. It was carried "across the shoulder like a pike or halberd", and its reach and weight were used to "force
polearms out of the way". Wikipedia's ōdachi page says its swordplay focused "on downward cuts", that "only a few
powerful men could use it effectively", and that its weights run 2.2–14.5 kg. Half-swording (the left hand gripping the
middle of the blade for "more forceful thrusts") gives a two-hander a short, close mode. It could become a RYOTE or
Bankai close-range variant. I found no primary ōdachi kata text, so the ōdachi entries rest on Wikipedia.

### 2. Oversized fictional blades on screen and how weight is sold

**Miura on the Dragon Slayer.** Miura's own view reaches us through the Berserk wiki's relay of the *Berserk Official
Guidebook* interview. He felt earlier big swords "were merely drawn big" and never "invoked realistic reactions upon
being swung". He wanted it to look as if "someone with the physique of a pro-wrestler may be able to swing it once or
twice". The wiki describes the sword as working "more like a bladed maul than an actual sword". Guts uses the flat to
"squash and flatten" foes and the width "in lieu of a shield". Editor Akira Shimada (Popverse) says the sword is "never
treated as if it is weightless", in contrast to Cloud. FF7 Remake director Hamaguchi admits that Cloud swinging the
Buster Sword "around with one hand" seemed to "weaken the sense of realism", and they kept it anyway.

**The Berserker Armour.** It suppresses "pain and fear, allowing them to bypass the limitations of their body". The
cost is self-harm "through overexertion" and a feral state that cannot tell "friend and foe". The helm becomes a biting
beast mouth. This is a close analogue for NOMIHOSE / Bankai: armour plus self-damage plus a bite or grab.

**Animation craft.**
- Timing: "more frames create slower, heavier movements". Anticipation "prepares the audience for a major action".
  Follow-through: "Nothing stops all at once" (WKU 12 principles).
- Japanese tametsume is 「原画寄りに中割を詰めて」 motion that gets 緩急やメリハリ (in-betweens bunched toward the key for speed contrast and accent).
- A smear frame is "used to simulate motion blur".
- On hitstop, Sakurai (Source Gaming translation): "the more damage an attack inflicts, the longer the hitstop", with a
  hard cap, and both sides freeze equally.
- critpoints notes that in Street Fighter "the cancel takes effect at the end of the hitstop".
- Monster Hunter Wilds' leads cut hitstop for the beta, and players asked for "a harder hit stop" to feel "the weapons
  hitting the monster" (MP1st).

**Cadence implication (my inference, not a source claim).** If the heavy forms add hitstop, every link and buffer window
must count from the end of hitstop. Otherwise the same input rhythm that works for the katana arrives early on the
cleaver or the club. That would be one concrete source of 「節奏不順」.

### 3. One-hand cleaver, axe and messer with a working free hand

Lecküchner's *Kunst des Messerfechtens* (1482; Żabiński–Mitchell–Fritz translation, Munich, Wiktenauer) is the core
source. It is the most direct model for KATATE.

- **The stance.** Fence "with one hand with the Messer and you should have the other one on the back". For empty-hand
  work, turn "the hand from the back to the chest". This gives a visible stance and tell: the free hand comes forward
  before a grab.
- **Free-hand techniques.** Many are paired with a chop or a hilt blow:
  - "hit with your left hand into his right elbow ... and strike with your Messer to his head";
  - "grab with your left hand behind his elbow on the outside and push him", then "drop your Messer" and throw at the
    knee;
  - "Grab the neck and learn to throw over the leg";
  - left hand on the middle of the blade, then "strike to him with the hilt at his head";
  - "hit ... with your right elbow into his Messer";
  - "push him from you with your left hand and take the balance at the elbow".
- **The Zornhau.** It "breaks all the strokes from above but is still nothing else but a simple peasant stroke". The
  master also teaches a counter "if one strikes to you with a great peasant stroke".

The Wikipedia Messer page confirms the weapon was single-handed, 45–90 cm, with a *Nagel* on the guard, and that larger
ones had a grip "suitable for a second hand-hold".

For the axe:
- **The beard.** It can "pull a weapon or shield out of the grasp of an enemy" (Wikipedia).
- **Saga combat.** Hurstwic's saga survey finds "an aggressive, direct fighting approach using simple moves". It includes
  an axe-horn jab after a sidestep and a head split "down to his shoulders".

Jack Chen (Chinese swordsmanship): the left hand "may be used to grab the enemy's sword-hand or just to push his weapon
aside", and "both arms are always moving".

Caveat: Wikipedia says the real falchion, though cleaver-shaped, was "not cleaving or chopping" but a quick slasher. A
cleaver form need not be slow; its weight shows in what it does on contact.

### 4. Fighting with one arm

**Clinical study.** The strongest biomechanical evidence is Nakamura & Okita's study of five transhumeral amputees
walking. Without a prosthesis:
- the trunk "rotated toward the intact side", and all patients swung the intact arm;
- the trunk leaned to the intact side;
- the rotation axis sat on the intact side.

Restoring arm mass made posture more symmetric. This is walking, not fighting, but it gives KATAUDE a grounded silhouette:
an asymmetric lean and twist toward the sword arm, with the turning axis off-centre.

**Film and history.**
- *One-Armed Swordsman* (1967): the hero loses his right arm and masters a one-armed style with "a broken sword" and "a
  half-burnt martial arts manual" (Wikipedia). Lau Kar-leung "designed a variety of signature moves for the one-armed
  swordsman, highlighting the tenacity of the character" (Hong Kong Memory). A broken blade plus one arm is exactly
  Kenpachi's Bankai-to-KATAUDE path.
- Tange Sazen lost his right eye and right arm (Wikipedia).
- Götz von Berlichingen lost his right hand at the wrist and "continued his military activities" with an iron hand
  (Wikipedia).

**Fiction with prosthetics.**
- Guts' prosthetic left hand hides "a small magnet in order to better handle the Dragon Slayer". He fires its cannon by
  "pulling the cord with his teeth" (Berserk wiki).
- In *Berserk: The Prototype* he swings the sword one-handed only.
- Miyazaki calls Sekiro's prosthetic a "multi-use feature" giving "a variety of moves that you can use in combat"
  (PlayStation Blog).

### 5. Biomechanics: swing speed versus weight

**Cross & Bower** (J. Sports Sci. 2006, measured), the most useful finding:
- At maximum effort, swing speed falls with swing-weight (moment of inertia) as V = C/I^n with n = 0.27 at high swing-weight
  (Io > 0.03 kg·m²); n ≈ 0.12–0.16 at low swing-weight (verifier's FIX).
- Speed "remained approximately constant as swing mass increased" when swing-weight was fixed.
- One- and two-handed swings "obey the same or very similar power laws".
- Forearm-only swings slow more as swing-weight rises.
- They cite earlier work: bat speed fell "only 10%" for a 1.6× rise in bat MOI.

My own arithmetic from their formula, not on the page: above 0.03 kg·m², doubling swing-weight costs about 17% of speed, and
kinetic energy still rises about 1.4× (less loss below it).

**The Kiel course script (Föll).** The sword can be swung "much faster than the axe". It advises preferring the axe
"whenever you have all the time in the world to get it up to speed".

**Design reading (inference).** Heavy forms should not get much slower active frames. Their weight belongs in:
- the wind-up (getting it "up to speed");
- hitstop on contact;
- whiff recovery (Figueyredo's fall on a miss);
- whole-body rotation instead of arm-only swings (the forearm penalty).

This is what lets the four heavy forms differ by where the cost sits, not by one slow multiplier:
- **KATATE:** short chop, cost in the free-hand setup.
- **NOMIHOSE:** wheel momentum, cost on a whiff.
- **Bankai:** long wind-up, huge hitstop.
- **KATAUDE:** spin-driven, opening after the spin.
